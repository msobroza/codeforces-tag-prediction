Each exercise comes with its statement and one accepted Python solution. The task statement asks for the eight focus tags; an exercise can have several, or none (46% have none). Every tag counts equally in the macro F1, so the rare tags such as probabilities and games weigh as much as math. Goals (PROBLEM.md): per-tag metrics, a command line that predicts and evaluates, and a prediction in under ten seconds. My constraint: everything trains and runs on one consumer GPU, an RTX 2080 Ti with 11 GB of memory. It shaped the methods: ModernBERT is tuned on one fold and the large model gets LoRA, F2LLM's 0.6-billion-parameter encoder stays frozen, and on the GPU every prediction goes one problem at a time. The data and the paper's baselines come from xCodeEval, by Khan and colleagues, published at ACL 2024 (arXiv 2303.03004).

Mostly one focus tag

Focus tags come with others

Over all 37 tags, a problem carries 2.8 tags on average, most often 2 or 3, up to 11. Over the 8 focus tags the picture changes: 2,304 problems carry none of them, 2,019 exactly one, 596 two, and only 63 three or more. So among the problems that have a focus tag, three quarters have exactly one, and the mean is 1.27. Focus tags rarely come alone: 89% of these problems also carry a non-focus tag such as greedy, implementation or dp, and the more focus tags a problem has, the more tags it has overall. The consequence for the models: each tag is a separate yes or no decision with its own threshold, there is no fixed number of tags to output, and predicting nothing is a valid and frequent answer.

A real pair from the data, described in my own words. Wonderful Coloring 1 and 2 are the easy and hard versions of one problem, released together in 2021: the easy one paints letters of a string with two colours and asks for a count; the hard one generalises to a sequence of integers with k colours and asks for the colouring itself. Their TF-IDF cosine is only 0.497, so a 0.8 threshold would miss them, while a 0.4 threshold catches them but chains unrelated problems into groups of up to 11. That is why candidates at 0.4 go to two independent LLM verdicts, here both "versions", and the pair becomes one group that lands whole in one dev fold. Note the tags: the easy version is tagged strings, the hard one has no focus tag at all, a reminder that labels are not fully consistent. Across the data, 82 pairs are such versions; a random split would put about 26 of them across train and test.

The first step finds problems that are versions of each other: TF-IDF on the descriptions proposes 203 pairs at cosine 0.4 or more, and two independent verdicts from Claude Opus 5.5 keep the 82 that are easy and hard versions of one problem; the verdicts agree on 484 of the 485 pairs they judged. Then iterative stratification, the method of Sechidis, Tsoumakas and Vlahavas from ECML PKDD 2011, places whole groups: it takes the rarest label first and puts each of its groups in the fold that lacks it most, balancing the 8 tags, the 9 pairs shared by 20 or more problems and the release year; problems without a focus tag are stratified apart, by year. The test fifth, 994 problems, is scored once, and mirrors the data: math is 28.3% of it as of all problems, and the worst tag, number theory, is 7.2% against 7.0%; the dev part is cut again into five folds the same way. Against 50 random splits, the frozen one leaks no pair (random by problem leaks 26 on average) and its worst tag is 3.1% off its share, against 28% for random group splits. The split is frozen in assets/split/split.csv.

Top lane: what happens once. The data is read and split, each method is tuned on dev out of fold only, then frozen: dev predictions out of fold, the test set scored once. A verify command re-derives every frozen file, MLflow mirrors it, and the CLI serves it. Bottom lane: one prediction with the TF-IDF model. 3 · Pinned in MLflow

How the TF-IDF method is built, simplified from the repository. Step one: a single scikit-learn Pipeline takes raw problems: its first step is the project's preprocessing, then the two TF-IDF feature blocks, then one linear SVM per tag. Because preprocessing lives inside the pipeline, the exact same object is used to train, to verify and to predict from the command line with --method tfidf. Step two: CrossFittedTagThresholds wraps the pipeline: it clones and fits it on four dev folds at a time to score the fifth, chooses each tag's threshold on those out-of-fold scores, then refits on all of dev; the result is saved with skops. Step three: the run record fingerprints what the result came from, the git commit, the dataset and split hashes, the environment and the settings, and log_method logs it once per recipe, in its own MLflow run, through a models-from-code wrapper, with its input signature, pinned requirements and parameters, then the dev and test metrics on the logged model. The freeze command refuses to write anything unless the new fit reproduces the study's out-of-fold F1 per tag exactly, its thresholds, and the pinned test macro F1; verify refits and re-derives the predictions, scores and method folder, and checks the run record's recipe.

Model selection never touches the test set (ADR 0001). Every method is compared on dev out-of-fold macro F1: fitted on four folds, scored on the fifth, with per-tag thresholds chosen on the other folds. Tuning happens only on dev: TF-IDF's 200-trial study, ModernBERT tuned on one fold because a trial takes 5 to 20 minutes, F2LLM's grid studies. The zero-shot methods learn nothing, and Jev learns only its eight thresholds. Then each frozen method scores the 994 test problems once; the next slides report those test scores.

From the simplest to the most recent. The label prior is the floor. TF-IDF is the tuned classical baseline, trained from scratch: 203 Optuna trials over statement and code features, ending on linear SVMs. ModernBERT is fine-tuned end to end, either with LoRA on the large model or fully on the base model, and bagged over the five folds. F2LLM keeps a 0.6-billion-parameter embedding model frozen and learns only a linear head, alone or fused with TF-IDF. The zero-shot methods send the xCodeEval paper's Code2Tag and DesCode2Tag prompts to OpenAI's gpt-6-luna, with no reasoning and temperature 0.32, close to the paper's 0.325, in free-text and JSON output. TypeSafe Jev is asked one yes/no question per tag, with a written definition of the tag, and its probabilities go through the same cross-fitted thresholds as every score method.

Adding the solution

Dev macro F1: 0.729 both. Test: 0.737 without, 0.751 with.

Jev is TypeSafe's model, pinned at version 1.13.0. It does not write text: it reads a state, here the problem's statement and, for jev-statement-code, its solution, and answers each question it is given with a probability. Each request asks eight Noul questions, one per focus tag, in one call. Every question carries the same convention, that Codeforces tags name the ideas of the intended solution and that a problem often has several, the same question, would Codeforces put this tag on this problem, and the tag's own criteria: a definition of what counts as yes with examples it includes, and of what counts as no with examples it excludes. Shown here is number theory: properties of integers count; an answer printed modulo a large prime only to keep it small does not. The definitions were written from Codeforces conventions, without reading a dev problem or answer, and frozen. Nothing else is learned. The answer is a probability by name only: the per-tag thresholds, chosen on dev out-of-fold scores and ranging from 0.335 to 0.895 for jev-statement-code, decide, not 0.5. On dev, adding the solution changes nothing measurable: 0.729 macro F1 either way, against 0.732 for descode2tag-json, the best on dev. On test, jev-statement scores 0.737 and jev-statement-code 0.751, reported and not used to choose. jev-statement-code is the CLI default because it is about five times faster than descode2tag-json, 0.26 s against 1.21 s, for about thirteen cents per thousand problems. The frozen scores come from the committed answers; a new call is a new sample, so live scores can drift slightly.

An executable contract

Every method's tests call check_tagging_method.

The contract every tagging method follows, simplified from methods/__init__.py. Both protocols are runtime-checkable, so the tests can check a method with isinstance. A TaggingMethod has a read-only name, its key in the registry, the tags it outputs, a predict that turns raw problems into 0/1 tags, and a save that writes its own data files. A score method, such as TF-IDF, the ModernBERT bags, F2LLM or Jev, also exposes the scores its per-tag thresholds are applied to. Each method is one line of the METHODS registry: the module that loads it, its kind, the packages and dependency groups it needs, and whether it calls a paid API. save_method writes method.json and the method's files; load_method reads method.json and imports the module the registry names, which is how the CLI and the MLflow wrapper load a method. And the contract is executable: every method's tests call check_tagging_method, which checks the output shapes, types and tag vocabulary, that the labels or extra columns change nothing, that one problem at a time gives the same as a batch, that saving and reloading gives the same output, exactly or within the score tolerance the method declares, that the folder holds only data, and that loading and predicting in a fresh interpreter imports neither MLflow, Optuna nor an LLM provider's SDK.

What we learned matters more than the scores. The statement carries most of the signal: the paper's prompts that see only the solution score 0.63 to 0.67 on test, against 0.72 to 0.74 with the statement. Full fine-tuning was not needed: frozen F2LLM embeddings under a linear head beat fine-tuned ModernBERT. Thresholds matter as much as models: the same untuned logistic regression goes from 0.19 macro F1 at a single 0.5 cutoff to 0.61 with per-tag thresholds, out of fold on dev. On the local RTX 2080 Ti, the dense-embedding methods (F2LLM) come close to Jev: late fusion scores 0.748 on test against Jev's 0.751, at 0.13 to 0.14 seconds a prediction against 0.26, about twice as fast, and for free; on dev they trail more, 0.706 against 0.729, which is why the choice made on dev stays Jev. The LLM-based methods gain mostly on graphs and number theory, by 0.11 to 0.18 over TF-IDF with intervals above zero, and not clearly elsewhere. Searching Jev's words did not help: GEPA rewrote the convention and the tag definitions on dev folds 1 to 3, chose on fold 4, and lowered the balanced Brier score it searched on, but the held-out fold shows no detectable gain in macro F1, 0.732 against 0.725, a paired +0.006 with an interval from -0.006 to +0.022; on the test set, a reference, it scores 0.727 against 0.751, mostly through probabilities, for $10.65 of requests. Better probabilities did not make better tags. Rare tags are noisy: probabilities has 18 test problems and an interval of about plus or minus 0.14. And asking for structured output helps: the same DesCode2Tag prompt scores 0.741 with a JSON schema against 0.721 as free text, and also leads on dev. In numbers: The best test macro F1 is 0.75, from TypeSafe Jev reading the statement and the solution; the methods that read the statement through an LLM all land between 0.72 and 0.75, ahead of fine-tuned ModernBERT and TF-IDF. The CLI default answers in a quarter of a second for about thirteen cents per thousand problems, and TF-IDF stays the free offline fallback. And the protocol: every choice was made on dev, out of fold; the test set was scored once per method.
